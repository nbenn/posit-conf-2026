1Any shape you want0:00 · 18s
Total freedom, but little help

Play-doh. It’s a fun toy: you can create any shape you want with it.

To me personally though, that much freedom always felt more like a burden. You don’t get much assistance from the material.

I gravitated more towards lego.

2What carries over to your next app?0:18 · 78s
Limit the shape, define the interface

Restrictions and well-defined interfaces helped me be more creative.

Two lumps of play-doh assemble however you press them together. Assembly of two bricks on the other hand is mediated by studs.

On the surface, lego costs you freedom. There are shapes you simply cannot build. What you get back is reuse, and with shaping constraints, you can spend your attention on the model instead of the material.

Much of this applies to what we do. I work at a small consultancy, and we have built a lot of Shiny apps over the years.

Every project felt like it needed to be tailor-made, so we ended up solving similar problems over and over again. Code bases grew large and hard to change. Clay, basically.

Now how can we improve? How can we have components carry over from one app to the next?

One way is by imposing restrictions. If we limit the shape by defining an interface, this enables reuse. It lets you focus on what matters. And it applies to humans and current-gen coding assistants alike.

3A block1:36 · 29s
Data inputs. User parameters. One data output.

The core abstraction we built for blockr is a block: a well-defined unit of computation.

Blocks are connected by links and can be assembled into stacks.

Each block has data inputs, user parameters and a single output. That is the whole contract.

The block is the brick, the link is the stud and the stack is the model. The stud is our interface: simple and well-defined.

4A real app2:05 · 65s
Bristol-Myers Squibb: up to 100 blocks across a dozen views, one created specifically for this use case.

We are using blockr at Bristol-Myers Squibb to explore clinical data from studies.

Our frontend here is built on a docking layout manager and we have every block render a panel you can move and resize freely.

These apps pull in up to one hundred blocks, spread over a dozen views. And we’re rolling out many such apps, specific to studies and their analysis needs.

Blocks are not the only extension point. There are extensions as well:

(1) On the left, we have a graphical overview, showing blocks, their connections and stack membership.

(2) And on the right, an AI assistant, which can manipulate and interrogate our app.

Most blocks here are general purpose: reading data, transforming it, displaying summary tables and visualizations that support drill-down.

(3) But one block was built specifically for this use case: the patient profile you can see in the middle.

Similar to specialty pieces that come with certain lego sets, sometimes you want a specific shape, which still comes with the standard studs.

We made it easy for you to do just that by integrating your own blocks into a blockr app. Here’s what one looks like.

Rehearsal plan

Five days from Thursday 10 to the talk on Tuesday 15 at 13:05. Thirty to forty minutes a day, in two or three short sessions rather than one long one. Sleep between sessions does part of the work.

What is being memorised

Every sentence, as written. The talk is 626 words in a hard five minutes, and at the marked pace it runs 4:43, so the slack is under twenty seconds. Improvised wording is what eats slack: a bridge made up on stage is always longer than the sentence it replaces. Fixed words are the timing.

Fixing the words does not mean learning them as a string. The words hold because the structure under them holds: each slide is a run of beats, each beat does one thing for the argument, and each sentence inside a beat is forced by the one before it. The beats are learned first, in your own words if need be, and the exact wording is fixed on top of them against the letters and the checks. That is how actors get a script word-perfect, and it is the opposite of the fade-out sheets, which drilled words without the structure.

A dot on the sheets, and ! in beats.md, marks a sentence where a slip would show: it echoes the slide, or it cues a click. Those are the ones to check first when time is short, not the only ones to get right.

The ladder

Eight views, one tab each. The unit is the slide throughout.

  • Full. Read aloud with the deck, twice. Then close it and tell the slide in your own words, to check that you hold the argument and not only the text. This is the one place your own words are the point.
  • Beats. One line per beat saying what it does. Say the beat from that line, then tap to check. This is where the sequence gets learned, so do not hurry past it. A purpose line that does not ring true gets rewritten in beats.md and regenerated: the line has to be yours.
  • Letters. One line per clause, every word as its first letter and the keyword whole. Recite as written; the letters are the running check, and a line you cannot read off is a line to tap and read again. From here up, a word not as written is a miss.
  • Clauses. The same lines, keyword only. Recite from the keywords.
  • Sentences. One keyword per sentence. Recite from those.
  • Joins. The deck. The end of a sentence in front, the next sentence behind, shuffled. Say the next sentence, show, then Next or Missed; a missed card goes to the back and comes round again. Filter to one slide while that slide is still new. The transition is what actually fails on stage, so this rung gets the most repetitions.
  • Starts. Draw a slide, a beat or a sentence and go on from there to the end of the slide. This checks that each cue reaches its content directly, without a run-up from the top.
  • Nothing. The slide picture, the clicker in hand. Tap the picture to check. The pocket check at the end is the whole talk as first letters, one sentence per line, for the day itself.

Paper is not a rung: the whole talk as written, one sentence per line, numbered as on the other sheets. Print it from its tab with the Print this tab button, two pages, and mark each slip on it during a run. After three runs the marks show whether the weak spots are the same ones or wander, and that decides the fix: the Joins deck on those sentences, or more of everything.

A rung is passed when you get through it twice in a row without a miss. On a miss, drop one rung, not to the bottom.

A miss is anything that would show on stage: a beat skipped or out of order, a sentence not as written, or a stall longer than a breath. On the Beats rung a beat said in your own words is not a miss, since that rung is about the sequence; from Letters upward it is. Pausing to think is not a miss while you are still on the sheets. Once you are running from nothing, a pause you would not choose to make on stage counts, because by then the words should arrive on their own and the pauses should be yours.

When a word will not come, go to the next sentence’s keyword and say that sentence. Never bridge with words of your own: the bridge is where the time goes, and a clean jump is invisible from the floor.

  • The closing invitation.
